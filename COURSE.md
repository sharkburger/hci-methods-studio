## Reference weekly sequence

### Week 1: Introduction & research foundations

Reference date: Sep 9, 2026

Reading, paradigms & research planning

Compare what different approaches accept as knowledge: interpretation, intervention, design exploration, or measurement. A method is a reasoned link between a question and evidence, not a checklist chosen after building.

**Approach:** Map a question, an intended contribution, and two plausible methods. Read a paper once for its structure, then for the argument and evidence.

**Produces:** A question–evidence map and a record of assumptions.

**Limits:** No single paradigm answers every question; reading an abstract does not establish a paper’s argument.

### Week 2: Interviews: ethnographic & phenomenological

Reference date: Sep 16, 2026

Qualitative / lived experience & situated practice

Ethnography investigates situated practices and meanings through field engagement. Phenomenological inquiry attends to how an experience is lived. Interviews can support either, but an interview alone is not automatically an ethnography.

**Approach:** Plan consent and access; ask open questions about concrete episodes; observe context where appropriate; write fieldnotes and reflexive memos; interpret accounts in relation to the question.

**Produces:** An interview guide, fieldnotes, and an interpretive account with supporting material.

**Limits:** Accounts are situated and shaped by the encounter; a short exercise cannot substitute for sustained fieldwork.

### Week 3: Case studies & historical research

Reference date: Sep 23, 2026

Qualitative / cases, archives & change

A case study investigates a bounded case in context using relevant sources. Historical inquiry examines change, continuities, and assumptions through records and artifacts. Both require an explicit account of source selection.

**Approach:** Define the case and time boundary; gather contrasting sources; record provenance; compare explanations; connect the case to a broader question.

**Produces:** A case narrative, timeline, and chain of evidence.

**Limits:** A case is not a population estimate; archival gaps and retrospective interpretation limit what can be claimed.

### Week 4: Grounded theory

Reference date: Sep 30, 2026

Qualitative / iterative theory construction

Grounded theory develops conceptual explanations through iterative data generation and analysis. Constant comparison, memoing, and theoretically motivated sampling connect emerging categories to further inquiry. Choose and explain a grounded-theory tradition.

**Approach:** Code early material, compare incidents and categories, write analytical memos, and let conceptual gaps inform what to investigate next.

**Produces:** An evolving category model with memos linking concepts to material.

**Limits:** A one-pass thematic sorting exercise is not a grounded theory study; sampling and theoretical development need time.

### Week 5: Action research & practice-based design research

Reference date: Oct 7, 2026

Applied / change through inquiry and making

Action research links inquiry to purposeful change with people in a setting. Practice-based design research develops knowledge through documented creative practice. The role of the researcher, collaborators, and artifacts must be explicit.

**Approach:** Agree on a problem and roles; plan an intervention or design exploration; act or make; observe consequences; reflect and revise; retain a decision trail.

**Produces:** Cycles of action and reflection, design artifacts, and a justified account of learning.

**Limits:** A successful artifact alone is not a research contribution. Change is contextual, and researcher participation affects the setting.

### Week 6: Participatory research

Reference date: Oct 14, 2026

Applied / participation, power & collaboration

Participatory research involves people in shaping inquiry and decisions, rather than only providing feedback. Participatory design uses collaborative activities and materials to make possibilities discussable. Consider whose expertise and interests are represented.

**Approach:** Map stakeholders and decision rights; co-define priorities; adapt activities for access; document decisions and disagreements; return interpretations for discussion.

**Produces:** A participation plan, co-design materials, and an account of shared and unequal decision-making.

**Limits:** Attendance at a workshop does not demonstrate shared power; representation, compensation, and ability to disagree matter.

### Week 7: Material cultural studies

Reference date: Oct 21, 2026

Applied / objects, meanings & material relations

Material cultural inquiry studies how objects participate in social practices, values, and relationships. Follow artifacts across making, use, repair, exchange, and disposal rather than treating materials as neutral containers for technology.

**Approach:** Choose an artifact or material; record its history and handling; combine observations, accounts, and documentation; examine meanings and relations.

**Produces:** An object biography or material-practice map supported by evidence.

**Limits:** Interpretations depend on context and access; material analysis does not reveal another person’s experience without inquiry.

### Week 8: Think-aloud & environmental behavior

Reference date: Oct 28, 2026

Usability + design / action, space & traces

Think-aloud asks people to verbalize during a task to reveal aspects of their interaction. Observation, shadowing, photo studies, and physical traces examine activity and its setting. Each makes a different slice of experience visible.

**Approach:** Specify the task or observation question; use neutral prompts; separate action records from interpretations; record context; identify problems and alternative explanations.

**Produces:** A task record, observation log, trace map, and prioritized design questions.

**Limits:** Verbalization can change the task; silence is not absence of thought. Traces do not uniquely identify intentions.

### Week 9: Design probes

Reference date: Nov 4, 2026

Usability + design / evocative inquiry

Probes invite people to respond through open-ended tasks, images, objects, or diaries. They can provoke interpretation and inspire design possibilities. Distinguish evocative probes from standardized measurement instruments.

**Approach:** State the exploratory intent; create accessible invitations; agree on sharing boundaries; discuss returned material; document how interpretations inform design.

**Produces:** A probe kit, returned materials, interpretive notes, and design directions.

**Limits:** Ambiguous responses should not be presented as representative survey results; disclose the role of designer interpretation.

### Week 11: Transition & futures

Reference date: Nov 18, 2026

Futures / systems, backcasting & responsibility

Transition-oriented design considers long-term change across interconnected systems. Backcasting starts from a desired future and works toward possible pathways. Futures work examines assumptions and values rather than predicting a single outcome.

**Approach:** Map a system and stakeholders; articulate contrasting futures; question whose future is preferred; work backward to possible actions; reflect on consequences.

**Produces:** A future scenario, system map, and backcasting pathway.

**Limits:** A scenario is not a forecast; normative choices and uncertainty must remain visible.

### Week 13: Surveys, statistics & experiments

Reference date: Dec 2, 2026

Quantitative / measurement & comparison

Surveys elicit structured responses from a defined population or sample. Experiments compare conditions while addressing alternative explanations. Statistical analysis connects design, measurement, assumptions, effect estimates, and uncertainty.

**Approach:** Define constructs and outcomes; select or develop instruments; pilot wording and procedure; plan sampling or assignment; specify analysis; report uncertainty and limitations.

**Produces:** A survey or experimental protocol, analysis plan, and interpretable results.

**Limits:** Convenience samples limit generalization. Statistical significance, preference, usability, and clinical benefit are different claims.

### Week 14: Poster session

Reference date: Dec 9, 2026

Communication / peer critique

A research poster makes the question, evidence, methodological choices, and limits available for discussion. It is a communication format rather than a distinct research method.

**Approach:** Show the evidence chain; invite a substantive challenge; record what needs clarification or revision.

**Produces:** A poster and a critique memo.

**Limits:** Visual polish cannot compensate for an unsupported claim.

**Reference milestones:** Poster session in the reference course

**Added connection to Binyan’s interests:** Explain both experiential evidence and measurement limits of an embodied interaction study.

### Week 15: Final project & research proposal

Reference date: Dec 16, 2026

Synthesis / research design

Bring the question, literature, approach, analysis, ethics, feasibility, and intended contribution into one coherent plan. The proposal should make its choices and limits reviewable.

**Approach:** Revise after critique; connect every planned observation or measure to the research question; specify remaining uncertainties.

**Produces:** Reference milestones: mini-project deliverables and individual research proposal.

**Limits:** A proposal describes planned inquiry; it does not imply that findings or health benefits have already been established.

**Reference milestones:** DUE: mini-project final deliverables; DUE: individual research proposal

**Added connection to Binyan’s interests:** Develop a proposal at the intersection of creative practice, embodied interaction, and health.
